01 — THE CITY

A city shaped by centuries of exchange

Situated along the eastern banks of the tidal Hooghly River, Kolkata developed from three riparian villages—Kalikata, Sutanuti, and Gobindapur—into the administrative capital of British India and the premier commercial emporium of Asia. Over two centuries of imperial governance left behind a monumental landscape of Palladian public offices, Corinthian colonnades, and grand domed halls that earned it the moniker "City of Palaces."

Yet the true essence of Kolkata lies far beyond imperial brick and stone. During the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the city served as the fertile crucible of the Bengal Renaissance. In its historic colleges, salons, and publishing lanes, thinkers such as Raja Ram Mohan Roy, Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar, Swami Vivekananda, and Rabindranath Tagore forged modern Indian literature, philosophy, and political consciousness.

Today, Kolkata retains an exceptional humanistic vitality. Its identity is anchored by the rhythmic ebb and flow of the Hooghly, the intellectual camaraderie of its historic coffee houses, Asia's oldest electric tramcars quietly traversing the Maidan, and spontaneous cultural dialogues (adda) that continue to define daily life in Bengal.

CHRONOLOGY

A BRIEF HISTORY

Five key milestones documenting the urban, political, and cultural evolution of Kolkata.

1690

Early colonial settlement

Job Charnock, agent of the English East India Company, secures trading rights along the Hooghly, establishing a riverside fortified post at Sutanuti.

1772

Calcutta becomes the capital of British India

Warren Hastings centralizes imperial administration, designating Calcutta the capital of the British Indian Empire and inaugurating an era of monumental civic architecture.

1911

Capital transferred to Delhi

At the Delhi Durbar, King George V proclaims the transfer of the imperial administrative capital to New Delhi, leaving Calcutta as India’s cultural and intellectual epicenter.

1947

Indian independence

With national independence and the Partition of Bengal, the city becomes capital of West Bengal, absorbing millions of refugees and reshaping its democratic identity.

2001

Official English spelling becomes Kolkata

The state legislature officially amends the colonial English spelling "Calcutta" to "Kolkata," honoring the historical Bengali pronunciation and heritage.

ARCHITECTURAL ORDERS

Form, Material & Spirit

Three spatial movements that shaped Kolkata’s built environment across three centuries.

01 / ORDER

Palladian Neoclassicism

Anchored around B.B.D. Bagh (Dalhousie Square), imperial architects transplanted European neoclassical ideals onto the alluvial plains of Bengal. Writers' Building, the General Post Office, and St. John’s Church employ Tuscan and Corinthian colonnades, massive rusticated brick basements, and shaded verandahs engineered to temper the subtropical monsoon.

02 / ORDER

Bengal Navaratna & Terracotta

Indigenous Bengali temple architecture reached its devotional pinnacle at Dakshineswar and Belur Math. Drawing upon the traditional curved thatched cornices (chala) and nine-spired (Navaratna) sanctum roofs, master artisans created brick and terracotta sanctuaries attuned to rural riverfront traditions.

03 / ORDER

Industrial Steel & Maritime Riverfront

Kolkata’s industrial identity remains inseparable from the Hooghly’s tidal channel. The 705-meter cantilever span of Howrah Bridge, engineered without intermediate river piers using high-tensile Indian alloy steel, stands as one of the twentieth century’s greatest structural achievements alongside historic warehouse docks.
