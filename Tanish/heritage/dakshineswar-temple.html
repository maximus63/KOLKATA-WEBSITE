DAKSHINESWAR KALI TEMPLE

Kolkata · 1855

Plate III: The three-tiered Navaratna sanctum and riverfront Shiva temples along the Hooghly at Dakshineswar.

“A nine-spired riverfront sanctuary that became the spiritual epicenter of nineteenth-century Bengal.”
HISTORY

In 1847, the wealthy philanthropic zamindar Rani Rashmoni was preparing for a pilgrimage to Varanasi by riverboat to seek blessings of the Divine Mother. On the eve of her departure, tradition relates that Goddess Kali appeared to her in a dream and commanded: “There is no need to go to Varanasi. Install my image in a beautiful temple on the bank of the Ganges, and I shall manifest myself there to accept your worship.”

Rani Rashmoni purchased twenty acres of land along the eastern bank of the Hooghly in Dakshineswar, combining parcels previously held by an Englishman, a Muslim graveyard, and local residents. Construction proceeded between 1847 and 1855 at a cost of 900,000 rupees. The sanctum was consecrated on the auspicious festival of Snana Yatra, 31 May 1855.

Shortly after the consecration, young Gadadhar Chattopadhyaya (later known as Sri Ramakrishna Paramahamsa) assumed priesthood at the temple. His intense spiritual experiences and broad humanistic dialogues with seekers from all creeds transformed Dakshineswar into an internationally renowned center of spiritual inquiry.

ARCHITECTURE

Materials: Traditional Bengali terracotta brick masonry with polished lime mortar (chunam), featuring ornate plaster mouldings and raised plinths.

Architectural Influences: Built in the traditional Navaratna (nine-spired) style of Bengal temple architecture. The design draws upon rural thatched Bengali huts (bangla chala) with gently curved eaves and cornices, crowned by symmetrical spires.

Structure: The central Bhavatarini temple stands on a high masonry platform measuring 46 feet square and rises over 100 feet in height across three tiers. Four spires crown the lower terrace, four crown the intermediate tier, and a majestic central tower caps the sanctum.

Decorative & Layout Details: Directly facing the river is a colonnaded flight of bathing ghats flanked by twelve identical Aat-chala (eight-eaved) shrines dedicated to Lord Shiva. The courtyard also houses the Radha-Kanta temple, the music pavilion (Nahabat), and the sacred grove (Panchavati).

SIGNIFICANCE

Dakshineswar was revolutionary in nineteenth-century Hindu society. Founded by a woman from the lower-caste Mahishya community, the temple defied orthodox caste hierarchies by welcoming seekers of all backgrounds into its sanctum.

It served as the crucible where Sri Ramakrishna practiced diverse spiritual paths—including Tantra, Vaishnavism, Advaita Vedanta, Islam, and Christianity—proclaiming the harmony of all faiths: “Joto mot, toto poth” (As many faiths, so many paths). It was here that he met Narendranath Datta, who later emerged as Swami Vivekananda.

NOTABLE FEATURES
01
Navaratna Bhavatarini Sanctum:

Enshrines the basalt stone idol of Goddess Bhavatarini standing on the chest of Lord Shiva atop a silver thousand-petaled lotus.

02
Twelve Riverfront Shiva Temples:

A symmetrical row of twelve shrines lining the river ghat, each enshrining a black stone Shiva lingam.

03
Sri Ramakrishna’s Room:

Preserves the sacred bed, photographs, and personal effects of the mystic overlooking the riverfront courtyard.

04
The Panchavati Grove:

A grove of five sacred trees (banyan, peepal, ashoka, bel, and amla) planted by Sri Ramakrishna for deep spiritual meditation.
