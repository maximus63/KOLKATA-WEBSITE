WRITERS' BUILDING

Kolkata · 1777

Plate X: The monumental red terracotta French Renaissance facade of Writers' Building along the northern edge of Lal Dighi.

“An imposing red-brick civic palace anchoring Dalhousie Square, bearing witness to imperial administration and revolutionary resistance.”
HISTORY

Originally constructed in 1777 by carpenter and builder Thomas Lyon for the British East India Company, Writers' Building was designed as living quarters and offices for junior administrative clerks known as "writers." Built on the northern bank of the ceremonial tank Lal Dighi, it originally presented a austere, unornamented neoclassical brick barracks.

In 1821, a monumental verandah with Ionic columns was added to protect the interiors from the intense tropical sun. Between 1879 and 1882, architect E.J. Martin carried out a dramatic transformation, casing the building in exposed red terracotta brickwork, ornate pediments, and mansard towers inspired by the French Renaissance.

On 8 December 1930, the building became consecrated in Indian freedom history when three young revolutionary nationalists—Benoy Basu, Badal Gupta, and Dinesh Gupta—entered disguised in European attire and assassinated the notorious Inspector General of Prisons, Col. N.S. Simpson. Following independence, the historic square (Dalhousie Square) was officially renamed Binoy Badal Dinesh Bagh (B.B.D. Bagh) in their eternal memory.

ARCHITECTURE

Materials: Deep red exposed terracotta brickwork complemented by ornamental stucco reliefs, rusticated base piers, cast-iron balustrades, and slate mansard roof caps.

Architectural Influences: French Renaissance Revival combined with Greco-Roman civic order, characterized by symmetrical facades, steep mansard roofs, and prominent central pediments.

Structure: An expansive linear edifice stretching 150 meters (nearly 500 feet) in length across B.B.D. Bagh North. It encompasses thirteen interconnected blocks with long internal corridors and soaring vaulted administrative halls.

Decorative Details: The parapet features monumental allegorical statues sculpted by British artist John Mossman, representing four foundational civic virtues: Science, Agriculture, Commerce, and Justice, flanked by an imposing central statue of Minerva.

SIGNIFICANCE

For over two centuries, Writers' Building (popularly known in Bengali as Mahakaran) served as the primary administrative engine of Bengal, functioning as the chief secretariat of the Government of West Bengal until recent comprehensive heritage conservation.

It anchors the UNESCO-recognized heritage precinct of Dalhousie Square, reflecting Kolkata's monumental political transitions: from trading company counting-house to imperial administrative powerhouse and, ultimately, to democratic sovereign state government.

NOTABLE FEATURES
01
150-Meter Terracotta Facade:

One of the longest continuous historic civic facades in India, faced in luminous red brick and classical pilasters.

02
Mossman Allegorical Sculptures:

Classical pediment figures representing Minerva, Justice, Commerce, Science, and Agriculture crowning the parapet.

03
The Historic B.B.D. Battle Corridor:

The first-floor administrative corridor where revolutionaries Benoy, Badal, and Dinesh engaged in the historic 1930 battle.

04
Lal Dighi Reflective Water Mirror:

The 25-acre freshwater tank in the center of Dalhousie Square offering reflective vistas of the red-brick palace.
